Overview

Great Learning Academy is a free learning platform for young professionals and college graduates. We spotted room for a premium, AI-powered tier on top of the existing free courses — job prep tools, guided projects, Microsoft and AWS certifications.

This required building a product brand from scratch that free users would trust enough to pay for. My job was to create the content foundation: the name, the positioning, the voice, and every word users would encounter on their path to subscribing.

The challenge

Content goals

  • Convince existing free users to upgrade
  • Create positioning for a brand-new product
  • Establish consistent tone across all platforms
  • Support marketing across emails, ads, and banners
  • Track content performance and iterate for phase 2

Constraints

  • Users were comfortable with the free product
  • Messaging had to be honest — zero gimmicks
  • AI features were still being built as copy was written
  • Needed to work across web, app, and email simultaneously

Naming the brand

The product was not always called Academy Pro+. It began as GLA Premium — a name that framed the upgrade as a paywall rather than a value proposition. Users didn't connect with it. After researching how brands like YouTube, Spotify, and Apple used the "+" symbol to signal genuine added value, we rebranded to Academy Pro+.

Case study 02 · Great Learning

Reducing Payment Failures Through Contextual Error Messaging

Redesigned payment failure states so the copy actually tells users what went wrong and what to do next — turning a dead end into a screen people could act on with some confidence.

Company
Great Learning Pvt Ltd
My role
Content Designer (Strategy + Execution)
Disciplines
UX writing, error state design, email, content strategy
Impact
+96% transactions YoY · −64% retry time

The problem

When a payment failed, every user saw the same generic message regardless of why their transaction was declined.

Original message: "Payment failed. Call support." — No reason. No next step. No recovery path.

The result: a ~90% failure rate on retries, a lot of abandoned transactions, and support tickets for things support couldn't actually fix. Users had no way to tell if the problem was their card, our platform, or the bank, so most just gave up and left.

User impact

  • No clarity on why the payment failed
  • No clear next steps or recovery path
  • High drop-offs after failure screens
  • Loss of trust in the platform

Business impact

  • ~90% payment failure rate
  • Lost revenue opportunities
  • Increased support dependency
  • Poor Lead-to-Enrolment (L2E) conversion

Key insight

The reframe

An error screen isn't just telling the system's side of the story — it's the moment a user decides whether to try again or walk away. People retry a lot more often when the copy tells them why it failed, doesn't dodge blame if it was our fault, and gives them an obvious next move.

Before vs. after

Before

Heading: Payment Failed

Message: Payment failed. Call support.

Generic. No reason given. One dead-end action. No trust rebuilt.

After

Heading: Try another mode of payment

Message: Your card was declined due to insufficient funds. Try another payment method or choose an instalment plan.

Clear reason. Actionable recovery. Reduced cognitive load.

Payment failure screens

Contextual failure — card-specific error + retry path

Redesigned generic failure — honest heading, clear CTA

Approach

1
Mapped all failure scenarios

Identified four core failure types: insufficient funds, card declined, technical failure, and gateway issues. For each, defined the cause, ownership (user vs. system), and the best next action.

2
Designed contextual error copy

Each failure type received a specific, honest message. Where the system was at fault, copy acknowledged it clearly — rebuilding trust instead of blaming the user.

3
Introduced recovery paths

Replaced dead-end screens with multi-CTA layouts: Retry payment → Switch method → Alternative gateway → Contact support (last resort).

4
Optimised international payment routing

Reduced friction with gateway routing: Visa/Mastercard → PayPal (higher success rate); AmEx/Other → Stripe. Content updated to guide users accordingly.

5
Extended recovery via triggered emails

Designed email templates that explained the failure reason, rebuilt trust after system errors, and included direct retry links — converting a lost transaction into an active recovery moment.

Case study 03 · Great Learning

SEO Meets UX: Redesigning Cybersecurity Pages for Intent-Driven Conversion

Rebuilt the content on our cybersecurity landing and product pages around what people were actually trying to find, instead of the keywords we wanted to rank for. Visitor-to-lead conversion and organic rankings both improved.

Company
Great Learning Pvt Ltd
My role
Content Designer (Strategy + Execution)
Disciplines
SEO content, UX writing, conversion optimisation, templates
Tools
SEMrush, SEO analytics

The problem

Content issues

  • Keyword-heavy but low on clarity
  • Poor alignment with user search intent
  • Repetitive messaging and keyword stuffing risk
  • Low engagement and suboptimal conversion

SEO issues

  • Strategy focused on ranking, not user value
  • Meta titles misaligned with click intent
  • No scalable content structure across pages
  • Content didn't answer what users were actually searching for

Objective

Goal

Improve Visitor → Lead (V2L) conversion, increase organic rankings for high-intent queries, and build a scalable content template that the team could apply across programme pages.

Approach

1
Shift to search intent

Mapped existing keywords to actual user questions. Reframed all content to answer in order: What is this course? Why should I care? Is it worth it? — matching the real decision journey of a prospective learner.

2
Built a scalable content template

Designed a reusable page structure: Value proposition → Outcomes → Proof points → Intent-driven FAQs. This ensured consistency across pages and gave the team a repeatable framework.

3
Balanced SEO with UX

Integrated keywords naturally into UI copy. Avoided keyword stuffing by ensuring each section delivered distinct value. Optimised meta titles and descriptions for click-through rate, not just keyword density.

4
Iteration and testing

Used SEMrush to track rankings and content performance. When an over-optimised experiment caused a ranking dip, rewrote meta content and reverted to intent-driven copy — rankings recovered within the same period.

5
Improved readability

Simplified overall content structure: short sentences, scannable sections, clear hierarchy. Reduced cognitive load so users could make decisions faster and with more confidence.

Version 1 vs. Version 2

The iteration from version 1 to version 2 reflects a deliberate shift — from a feature-heavy, text-dense layout to an outcome-driven, scannable page that guides users toward a decision.

Outcome-driven, structured hierarchy

Version 2

Reiterated programme page after preliminary research

SEO learnings and recovery

Initial dip

An over-optimised keyword experiment temporarily hurt rankings. Meta titles were misaligned with user click intent.

Recovery

Intent beats keywords. Ranking for the right queries matters only if the page answers what the user actually came to find.

Case study 05 · Great Learning

Claude Skill: Setting up an up-to-date skill in Claude for the Design Team

Great Learning started moving toward an AI-first way of working, and content was one of the first places that showed up. I set up a skill in Claude that holds our style guide and platform rules, so any writer or designer can check a page against them without waiting on someone senior to eyeball it.

Company
Great Learning Pvt Ltd
My role
Lead Content Designer
Disciplines
Prompt design, content governance, AI tooling, workflow design
Audience
Designers and writers across Olympus, the GL App, Academy Pro+, and degree programme pages

Overview

Great Learning's biggest challenge in content has always been the inconsistency in the final language, tone and grammar, which we adhered to. I set up a detailed skill in Claude that used our existing successful pages across platforms so that any writer or designer can review a page without waiting for someone to manually do the task.

Core objective

We already had a content design system in place (check case study 4), but the challenge was having the team implement it. Scanning through the content for large landing pages or program pages took effort, and not everyone found it easy to scroll through different sections on the design system. Therefore, we inputted the design into Claude and had the AI tool identify the core guidelines that the team had to follow.

The problem: too many small inconsistencies — casing, character limits, tone drift — were slipping through simply because a review took too long to fit into the sprint.

The Process

I built this as a Claude project rather than a one-off prompt, mainly so the reference material — our AP-based style guide, the component rules from the content design system, and platform-specific requirements — could live in one place and get reused across chats instead of being re-explained every time.

Project setup
The Claude project holding the skill, instructions, and reference material

The trickier part wasn't the style rules themselves — it was that "content review" means different things depending on which platform you're reviewing. A course card on Olympus has different content rules than a landing page or a degree program page. So before the skill can actually check anything, it needs to know what it's looking at. So I requested Claude to ask the user what platform they wished to review before getting started.

Intake questions
Working out which questions the skill should ask before reviewing
Mapping out platform-specific surfaces so the rules stay relevant to what's being reviewed

So the skill starts by asking: is this for Olympus, the GL App, a landing page, a programme page, or a marketing piece? Then it narrows further — who's the audience, college students or mid-career professionals — because the tone and vocabulary we'd use for each are genuinely different, and treating them the same was part of the original problem.

How it works

1
Upload and answer a few questions

A designer or writer drops in the page copy and tells the skill what it's for and who it's reaching.

2
Claude scans against the rules

Character limits, casing conventions, grammar, and the AP-based style points we've standardised on all get checked at once.

3
You get a flagged list, not a rewrite

The output points out where something's off and why, so the writer still owns the fix — this was meant to catch errors, not replace judgement.

Sample output
A review of an Olympus course page — flagged issues, a short summary, and what's already working

One thing I hadn't fully expected: the summaries at the top of each review turned out to be almost as useful as the flagged list itself. They tell you in a couple of sentences whether a page has one bad section or a systemic problem, which matters when you're deciding whether to fix three lines or start over.

Why this matters

It closes the gap between having a style guide and someone actually checking against it. The rules existed before; now they're something you can run a page through instead of memorise.

Newer writers don't have to wait on a senior reviewer to learn the voice. They can self-check first and come to review time with fewer basic issues already sorted.

It's still evolving. I'm adding platform rules as new surfaces come up, and I'd like to eventually fold in a rough SEO check — but I'm deliberately not trying to make it do everything at once.
